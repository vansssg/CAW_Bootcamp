STEP 7: REFLECT

Guided Reflection

Ask the student these questions. ‍​‍Let them think and respond — do not rush this.

​​​"Before this module, how did you think about risk in software projects? ​​​Has that changed?"

​​​Listen for whether they have moved from a vague sense of "this is hard" to a concrete framework. ​​‌The vocabulary matters — can they name a risk type and explain what makes it different from the others?

​‌​"Which risk type do you think you are most likely to underestimate in a real project? ‌​‌Why?"

​‌​Most students will say integration risk or scale risk, because those depend on external factors. ‌‌‌Accept any honest answer that shows self-awareness.

​‌​"What surprised you about the payment blocker scenario? ‌​​Did you expect the risk to be non-technical?"

​​​The lesson here is that risk assessment is not just a technical exercise. ‌​​The best risk-ordered plan in the world fails if you only look at code-level risks and ignore business, legal, and organizational blockers. ​​​A senior engineer thinks about all of these.

‌​‌"If you were starting a brand new project tomorrow, what is the first thing you would do before writing any code?"

​​​Ideal answer: identify the biggest unknown and test it. ​​‌Drill the test well. ​‌‌Run a spike. ​‌‌Do not build the pipeline before you know there is oil.

​​​Quality Assessment

Evaluate the student on:

- Risk identification quality: Did they find real risks, or did they just label easy things as risky? ‌‌‌Did they distinguish risk from difficulty?
‌​​- Risk type diversity: Did they use multiple risk types, or did everything get labeled the same way? ​​‌A plan with only one risk type suggests shallow analysis.
‌​‌- Response to non-technical risk: When the payment blocker was revealed, did they try to solve it technically (wrong instinct) or recognize it as a business problem (right instinct)? ‌‌‌Did they adapt the plan or freeze?
​‌‌- Plan restructuring quality: Is the updated plan coherent? ‌‌​Does the stubbed interface make sense? ​‌​Did they think about team flow and not just their own work?

‌​‌Decision Callback

In the DECIDE step, students chose between using the four provided risk types as-is or customizing the framework, and between running a spike first or building the riskiest item for real.

​​​If they chose A (spike first):
- The challenge they encountered was a consequence of a spike's limited ability to surface business-layer blockers
- Connect it: "A spike on Stripe integration would have produced working test-mode code — but the payment blocker was not a Stripe problem. ​​‌The client had no merchant account. ​​​A 2-hour technical spike cannot reveal that the business infrastructure does not exist yet. ​​‌Scouting the code does not scout the organization"

If they chose B (build for real first):
- The challenge they encountered was a consequence of investing in production code before confirming business preconditions
- Connect it: "Building a proper payment integration with error handling, tests, and edge cases only to discover there is no Stripe account to connect it to is exactly the scenario where 'build for real' becomes expensive. ​‌​When the unknown is outside the codebase, production-quality code does not protect you from it"

Ship Recap (1 Minute)

- What we shipped: a risk-annotated dependency graph with a numbered, justified build order that front-loads the scariest unknowns while respecting hard dependencies.
‌​​- What we learned: risk is not the same as difficulty. ‌‌‌Four types of risk (integration, novelty, dependency, scale) give us a concrete vocabulary. ​‌​Not all risks live in the codebase — business blockers are real and need to be planned around, not solved with code.
‌​​- Remaining risk: we have an ordered plan, but we have not sliced it into buildable, testable increments yet. ‌‌‌A risk-ordered list is better than an unordered list, but each item is still too big to hand to a developer (or an AI agent) and say "go build this." ​​​That is what Module 4 — Vertical Slicing — is for.

Dimension definitions (from manifest):
- requirement_extraction: Requirement Extraction - Evaluates whether the student identifies explicit, implicit, and conflicting requirements from ambiguous prose — including constraints, edge cases, and unstated assumptions.
- dependency_thinking: Dependency Thinking - Evaluates the student's ability to identify what blocks what, model dependencies as a DAG, find critical paths, and recognize when circular dependencies indicate a design problem.
- risk_assessment: Risk Assessment - Evaluates whether the student identifies technical unknowns, integration risks, and external dependencies early — and orders work to retire risk before committing to paths that depend on it.
- task_clarity: Task Clarity - Evaluates whether task specifications are precise enough for another person (or AI agent) to execute without asking questions — including clear scope, acceptance criteria, and interface contracts.
- adaptation_speed: Adaptation Speed - Evaluates how quickly and effectively the student responds to changing requirements — re-scoping, re-prioritizing, and communicating impact without starting over or panicking.
- integration_judgment: Integration Judgment - Evaluates the student's ability to bring parallel workstreams together — identifying merge conflicts, contract violations, and emergent bugs that only appear when components meet.
